Three key messages

What this group needs to understand

01

Common, treatable, never a personal failing

10.5% of 11-17 year olds meet the criteria for major depressive disorder. Young Australians rank it the 2nd most urgent national issue.

02

Help-seeking is a skill, not a weakness

Three of four affected adolescents never access professional help. We teach who to approach, what to say, what happens next.

03

Every student is a first responder

Young people disclose to friends, not adults. Bully-victims carry 4.3× the risk of suicidal ideation. Bystanders matter.

Lived-experience stories reduce stigma and raise help-seeking intentions.
Friends notice first

The strategy

Whole-school. Universal. Tiered.

records screened by the rapid review for the NSW Department of Education

The most effective programs were universal and whole-school, with age-specific content in sequence. Stand-alone programs such as the Resourceful Adolescent Program produced no significant reduction.

How the content is sequenced

Three stages. One continuous line.

Years 5-6 · Name it

Emotional literacy

Spot persistent low mood. Learn to ask for help. No suicide content at this stage.

Rehearses: managing big emotions

Years 7-9 · Understand it

Symptoms, causes, coping

Depression symptoms, causes and treatability. Coping strategies and spotting distress in peers.

Rehearses: handling school and social stress

Years 10-12 · Respond safely

Safe peer response

Suicide prevention inside mental health promotion, as headspace advises: no stand-alone suicide lessons, which prevents copycat behaviour.

Rehearses: responding when a friend is struggling

Role-play builds on YAM, an international program whose randomised trial halved suicide attempts at 12 months across 11,110 students. It refined our ideas for this age group and has been trialled in Australian schools.
(headspace, n.d. · Wasserman et al., 2015 · McGillivray et al., 2021 · Calear et al., 2022)

The mechanisms

What makes it stick

Strengths first

Every tier opens with a strengths assessment. Comparable one-off workshops report only satisfaction ratings; ours stays in-curriculum.

teen Mental Health First Aid

Years 7-12 complete three 75-minute peer-support sessions. An Australian randomised trial found improved first-aid intentions and confidence at 12 months.

Student-led campaign

Student leaders run the awareness campaign every August, anchored to Bullying No Way Week.

A wellbeing plan card

Warning signs, two chosen strategies, one trusted adult. Revisited yearly. Three habits, not a slogan: sleep, exercise, grounding.

Sleep is the named pillar: close to 60% of 16-17 year olds with anxiety or depression symptoms miss the minimum guidelines.
(MyStrengths, n.d. · Hart et al., 2022 · Bullying. No Way!, n.d.)

Implementation

Realistic. Feasible. Measured.

  • Curriculum time. Runs inside existing PDHPE lessons. No extra timetabling.
  • Existing staff. Starts in NSW government schools with current counsellors and Student Support Officers. Aligned with the National Suicide Prevention Strategy.
  • Safe materials. Mindframe guidelines: method and location kept private, 24/7 support lines always listed.
  • Staff training. Free Be You portal for teachers; Youth Mental Health First Aid for at least 10% of support staff.
  • Parents. Informed, an opt-out, and one information evening per stage.

Staged disclosure: no single counsellor absorbs it all

  1. 1

    First contact. Classroom teachers and Student Support Officers listen first.

  2. 2

    Sorting. Year Advisers and the Head Teacher Wellbeing triage by seriousness.

  3. 3

    Escalation. School counsellors step in for the most serious cases.

Measured yearly with PHQ-2. Judged at six years: 40,149 students, no significant effect through year 3, improvement across every outcome only from year 6.
(NSW Department of Education, 2025 · Everymind, n.d. · Be You, n.d. · Mental Health First Aid Australia, n.d.)
